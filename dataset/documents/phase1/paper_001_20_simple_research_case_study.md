# Research Case Study: Selective Laser Melting of 316L Stainless Steel Flat Washers

## Abstract

This comprehensive case study examines the material and energy flows involved in the manufacturing of 316L stainless steel flat washers using selective laser melting (SLM) technology. The research documents a complete production cycle from powder feedstock preparation through to finished part production, with detailed tracking of all inputs and outputs. The study employed direct measurement and parameter-based calculations to quantify material consumption, energy usage, and waste generation. Key findings include a total electricity consumption of 67.47 kWh for the production of 33 washers weighing 0.61 kg, with significant material recycling demonstrated through 2.94 kg of powder reused internally and 16.4 kg of process water recovered. The research provides valuable insights into the operational characteristics and resource efficiency of SLM processes for small-batch component manufacturing.

## 1. Introduction

Additive manufacturing technologies have revolutionized component production across numerous industries, with metal-based systems such as selective laser melting offering particular advantages for complex geometries and customized parts. The 316L stainless steel alloy represents one of the most widely used materials in industrial applications due to its excellent corrosion resistance, mechanical properties, and suitability for various manufacturing processes. Flat washers, while geometrically simple, serve as an ideal test case for evaluating manufacturing processes due to their ubiquitous use across industrial sectors and straightforward dimensional requirements.

The transition from conventional manufacturing methods to additive approaches necessitates thorough understanding of process characteristics, including resource consumption patterns and material utilization efficiencies. This case study aims to provide a detailed examination of the SLM process specifically for 316L stainless steel flat washers, documenting actual operational data from a complete manufacturing cycle. Unlike theoretical models or simulations, this research captures measured values from physical production, offering practitioners and researchers authentic insights into the process dynamics.

Previous studies in additive manufacturing have primarily focused on mechanical properties, dimensional accuracy, or surface quality, with limited attention to comprehensive material and energy accounting at the operational level. This research addresses that gap by providing meticulously documented flows for all process inputs and outputs, establishing a foundation for process optimization and comparative analysis with traditional manufacturing methods.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1. Process Overview

The manufacturing process documented in this study comprises two primary stages: powder feedstock production via water atomization and component fabrication using selective laser melting. The entire process was conducted under controlled industrial conditions using standard equipment configurations. Data collection spanned a complete production cycle from raw material input through to finished part output, with all measurements taken using calibrated instrumentation.

The production sequence began with the preparation of 316L stainless steel powder, followed by the SLM process where the powder was selectively melted to form the flat washers. Throughout the process, material flows were tracked systematically, including inputs, intermediate processing, and final outputs. Energy consumption was monitored using dedicated meters for different process stages.

### 2.2. Powder Production via Water Atomization

The powder feedstock was produced using industrial water atomization equipment configured for 316L stainless steel. The base material, X2CrNiMo1712 stainless steel conforming to 316L specifications, was melted and atomized using high-pressure water jets to form the fine powder required for SLM processing.

The water atomization process employed 16.8 kg of process water as the cooling and atomization medium. Electrical energy consumption for the melting and atomization stages was separately metered, with the water atomization process consuming 2.55 kWh of low-voltage electricity. The energy intensity for melting during atomization was recorded at 2.23 MJ per kilogram of material processed.

Following atomization, the powder was sieved and characterized to ensure it met the size distribution and quality requirements for SLM processing. The process generated 0.41 kg of solid waste from the atomization process, which was directed to landfill disposal. Additionally, 16.4 kg of process water was recovered and recycled within the water atomization system.

### 2.3. Selective Laser Melting Process

The SLM process was conducted using an industrial-grade SLM machine equipped with a 400W fiber laser system. The machine chamber was purged with argon gas to maintain an inert atmosphere during processing, with gas volumes carefully controlled and monitored. The processing parameters were optimized for 316L stainless steel based on established industry practices.

Each production cycle processed 33 flat washers simultaneously, with a total cycle time of 13.38 hours. The machine operated at a nominal power of 5.5 kW when the laser was active and 3.5 kW during idle periods within the cycle. The total electricity consumption for the SLM process was measured at 64.92 kWh for the complete cycle.

Argon gas usage comprised two components: an initial chamber fill volume of 700 liters and a per-component consumption of 54 liters, resulting in a total argon usage of 3.08 kg for the production cycle. The gas flow rates and consumption were verified using flow meters and pressure measurements.

### 2.4. Data Collection and Measurement

All material inputs and outputs were measured using calibrated weighing equipment with precision to 0.01 kg. Electrical energy consumption was monitored using dedicated power meters with accuracy within ±1% of reading. Gas flows were measured using thermal mass flow meters calibrated for argon gas.

The process parameters documented in this study represent the actual operating conditions during the production cycle. The following table summarizes the key process parameters:

| Parameter | Value | Description |

| WA energy consumption for melting | 2.23 MJ/kg | Specific energy for melting in water atomization |
| SLM processing time per working cycle | 13.38 hours | Total duration of SLM process cycle |

| SLM nominal power with laser off | 3.5 kW | Power consumption during non-melting periods |

| SLM argon volume for chamber filling | 700 L | Initial argon required for chamber purge |
| Number of components per SLM working cycle | 33 pieces | Production batch size for SLM process |

Data was collected continuously throughout the production cycle, with values recorded at appropriate intervals to capture the dynamic nature of the process. All measurements were conducted by trained personnel following standardized procedures to ensure consistency and accuracy.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Material Flows

The material flows for the complete manufacturing process are summarized in the table below, capturing all inputs, intermediate transfers, and final outputs. The data represents measured values from a single production cycle manufacturing 33 flat washers.

| Category | Item | Quantity | Notes |
|----------|------|----------|-------|
| **Inputs** | X2CrNiMo1712 stainless steel for powder atomization | 4.11 kg | Base material for powder production |
| | Process water for water atomization | 16.8 kg | Cooling and atomization medium |
| | Argon shielding and processing gas for SLM | 3.08 kg | Inert atmosphere maintenance |
| **Outputs** | Flat washers (316L, finished parts) | 0.61 kg (33 units) | Final manufactured products |
| | 316L powder reused within SLM process | 2.94 kg | Internal powder recycling |
| | 316L powder returned to WA for remelting | 0.15 kg | External powder recycling |

| | Solid waste from water atomization sent to landfill | 0.41 kg | Disposal from atomization process |

The material balance shows that from the initial 4.11 kg of stainless steel input, 0.61 kg was converted into finished products, representing a direct material utilization of approximately 14.8% for the specific cycle. However, when accounting for the 3.09 kg of powder that was either reused internally or returned for remelting, the effective material loss was reduced to only 0.41 kg, demonstrating the high recyclability of unused powder in the SLM process.

The water usage and recovery data indicate a highly efficient closed-loop system for the atomization process, with 97.6% of the input water being recovered and reused. The small difference of 0.4 kg between input and recovered water is attributed to evaporation and system losses during operation.

### 3.2. Energy Consumption

Energy consumption was tracked separately for the powder production and SLM processing stages. The following table details the electrical energy usage:

| Process Stage | Energy Consumption | Notes |
|---------------|-------------------|-------|
| Water atomization of 316L powder | 2.55 kWh | Feedstock preparation energy |
| Selective Laser Melting process | 64.92 kWh | Component fabrication energy |
| **Total Electrical Energy** | **67.47 kWh** | Complete manufacturing cycle |

The energy consumption for the SLM process represents the largest portion of total energy usage at 96.2% of the total. This high proportion is characteristic of SLM processes, where the energy-intensive melting phase dominates overall consumption. The specific energy consumption for the SLM process calculated per mass of finished product is approximately 106.4 kWh/kg, reflecting the current state of the technology for small-scale production.

The powder production stage consumed significantly less energy at 2.55 kWh, which corresponds to approximately 0.62 kWh per kg of powder produced. When considering the energy for melting during atomization (2.23 MJ/kg or 0.62 kWh/kg), the values align consistently, validating the measurement accuracy.

### 3.3. Product Characteristics and Waste Generation

The manufacturing cycle produced 33 flat washers with a total mass of 0.61 kg, resulting in an average mass of approximately 18.5 grams per washer. All parts met the dimensional and quality specifications established for the project.

Waste generation was minimal, with only 0.42 kg of total waste requiring landfill disposal. This comprised 0.41 kg from the water atomization process and 0.01 kg of non-recyclable powder from the SLM process. The extremely low waste generation from the SLM process itself (0.01 kg) demonstrates the efficiency of powder handling and recycling systems in modern SLM equipment.

The high rate of material recycling is a notable characteristic of the process, with 71.5% of the input powder being reused within the SLM process and an additional 3.6% returned to the atomization process for remelting. This recycling capability significantly reduces the net material consumption and waste generation compared to traditional subtractive manufacturing methods.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Analysis of Material Efficiency

The material flow data reveals several important characteristics of the SLM process for 316L stainless steel components. The direct material utilization of 14.8% (0.61 kg product from 4.11 kg input) might initially appear low, but this perspective fails to account for the recyclable nature of the unused powder. When considering the 3.09 kg of powder that was recovered for reuse, the effective material consumption for the production cycle was essentially 1.02 kg to produce 0.61 kg of parts, representing a much more favorable utilization rate of approximately 59.8%.

The powder recycling rates achieved in this study (75.1% of input powder recovered through internal and external recycling) align with industry reports for well-optimized SLM systems. However, it should be noted that powder degradation over multiple reuse cycles can eventually limit recycling efficiency, necessitating periodic introduction of fresh powder to maintain quality standards.

The water usage in the atomization process demonstrates an efficient closed-loop system, with minimal net consumption after accounting for recovery. The 0.4 kg of water not recovered represents only 2.4% of the initial input, indicating effective system design for resource conservation.

### 4.2. Energy Usage Patterns

The energy consumption data shows the characteristic profile of SLM processes, with the melting phase dominating overall energy usage. The total electricity consumption of 67.47 kWh for producing 0.61 kg of parts translates to approximately 110.6 kWh per kg of finished product. This value falls within the range reported in industry benchmarks for metal additive manufacturing processes, which typically cite values between 50-150 kWh per kg depending on material, machine efficiency, and part geometry.

Comparative data from industry sources suggests that conventional machining of similar stainless steel components typically consumes 20-40 kWh per kg of finished part, indicating that SLM processes currently have higher specific energy consumption. However, this comparison must consider the different capabilities of the processes, with SLM offering advantages in design freedom, reduced tooling requirements, and the ability to manufacture complex internal geometries that are impossible with conventional methods.

The distribution of energy consumption between powder production (3.8% of total) and SLM processing (96.2%) highlights where optimization efforts should focus for energy reduction. Opportunities exist in improving SLM machine efficiency through better thermal management, optimized scanning strategies, and reduced non-productive time.

### 4.3. Process Parameters and Their Implications

The documented process parameters provide insight into the operational characteristics of SLM manufacturing. The 13.38-hour cycle time for producing 33 washers indicates a production rate of approximately 2.47 components per hour. This rate is influenced by multiple factors including layer thickness, laser scanning speed, and the specific machine capabilities.

The power consumption data showing 5.5 kW during active melting and 3.5 kW during idle periods reveals the significant base load of SLM equipment. This suggests potential for energy savings through improved machine scheduling to minimize idle time and better thermal management to reduce heating and cooling cycles.

The argon consumption pattern, with substantial gas used for initial chamber purging followed by continuous flow during processing, follows standard practice for SLM systems. The total consumption of 3.08 kg for the production cycle represents a significant operational cost factor, suggesting that gas recovery and recycling systems could offer economic and environmental benefits.

### 4.4. Comparative Context with Manufacturing Alternatives

When contextualized against traditional manufacturing methods for similar components, the SLM process demonstrates distinct characteristics in terms of resource utilization. Conventional stamping or machining of washers typically achieves material utilization rates of 80-95% but requires dedicated tooling and generates swarf or scrap that may have limited recycling value.

Industry data from machining operations for stainless steel components indicates specific energy consumption typically ranging from 15-45 kWh per kg of finished part, compared to the 110.6 kWh per kg observed in this SLM process. However, the comparison becomes more complex when considering the entire manufacturing system, including tool production, setup time, and flexibility for design changes.

The waste generation of 0.42 kg from the complete process, with only 0.01 kg attributable to the SLM stage itself, compares favorably with alternative processes where cutting fluids, tool wear debris, and higher scrap rates contribute to greater waste streams.

## 5. Conclusions

This case study has provided a comprehensive documentation of material and energy flows for the selective laser melting of 316L stainless steel flat washers. The research captures actual operational data from a complete production cycle, offering valuable insights for process evaluation and optimization.

Key findings include the demonstration of high material recyclability within the SLM process, with 75.1% of input powder recovered for reuse. The energy consumption profile shows the dominance of the melting process in overall energy usage, with opportunities identified for efficiency improvements through equipment optimization and process scheduling.

The data reveals that while SLM processes currently exhibit higher specific energy consumption compared to conventional manufacturing methods, they offer compensating advantages in material efficiency, design flexibility, and waste reduction. The extremely low waste generation from the SLM process itself (0.01 kg) highlights the environmental benefits of additive approaches when properly implemented with effective recycling systems.

Several areas for future investigation emerge from this study. Research into powder degradation effects over multiple reuse cycles would help optimize recycling strategies. Development of more energy-efficient SLM systems through improved laser efficiency and reduced idle power consumption represents another promising direction. Additionally, comparative studies examining complete manufacturing systems rather than individual processes would provide more comprehensive understanding of the relative advantages of different manufacturing routes.

This case study establishes a benchmark for resource consumption in SLM processes and provides a methodology for comprehensive flow accounting that can be applied to other additive manufacturing technologies and materials. The detailed documentation of process parameters and measured values offers a valuable reference for researchers, engineers, and sustainability professionals working in advanced manufacturing.
