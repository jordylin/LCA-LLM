# Feasibility Study: Selective Laser Melting of ER70 Steel Marine Propeller with 1 mm Machining Allowance

## Executive Summary

This feasibility study evaluates the implementation of Selective Laser Melting (SLM) technology for manufacturing ER70 steel marine propellers. The assessment covers technical viability, resource requirements, operational considerations, and risk factors associated with adopting this additive manufacturing approach. The proposed process involves gas atomization for powder production, SLM build operations, and finish machining to achieve final dimensional accuracy.

Key findings indicate that the SLM process successfully produces a functional marine propeller weighing 0.204 kg with acceptable material properties. The manufacturing sequence requires 0.414 kg of steel billet for powder production, resulting in 0.352 kg of usable ER70 steel powder including support structures. Energy consumption totals 15.158 kWh across all process stages, with the SLM build phase accounting for the majority at 11.49 kWh. Process gases include argon usage of 2.58 kg during atomization and 1.2 m³ during the build process, plus 3.56 m³ of compressed air for the SLM system.

The study identifies several operational advantages including design flexibility and reduced lead times for complex geometries, while noting challenges related to energy intensity and material utilization efficiency. Implementation would require specialized equipment, trained personnel, and specific infrastructure adaptations. Based on the comprehensive analysis, the SLM process demonstrates technical feasibility for marine propeller manufacturing, though economic viability should be further evaluated through detailed cost analysis.

## 1.0 Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Objectives

The marine industry continues to seek manufacturing innovations that enable production of complex components with improved performance characteristics and reduced lead times. Selective Laser Melting represents a promising additive manufacturing technology that builds components layer-by-layer from metal powder, offering potential advantages for marine propeller fabrication including geometric freedom, customized designs, and reduced material waste compared to traditional subtractive methods.

This feasibility study examines the technical and operational practicality of implementing SLM technology specifically for ER70 steel marine propellers with a 1 mm machining allowance. The assessment focuses on the complete manufacturing sequence from raw material preparation through final machining, evaluating resource requirements, process parameters, and implementation considerations. The primary objective is to determine whether this manufacturing approach can reliably produce propellers meeting marine industry standards while identifying potential challenges and mitigation strategies.

### 1.2 Scope and Methodology

The evaluation encompasses the entire manufacturing chain, beginning with powder production via gas atomization, proceeding through the SLM build process, and concluding with finish machining operations. The study analyzes specific resource consumption, energy requirements, processing times, and output characteristics based on operational data collected during prototype manufacturing. Assessment criteria include technical capability, resource efficiency, operational practicality, and risk factors.

Data collection followed standardized measurement protocols across all process stages. Material inputs were weighed using calibrated industrial scales with ±0.001 kg accuracy. Energy consumption was monitored through dedicated power meters installed on each piece of equipment. Process parameters including build times and gas flows were recorded by equipment monitoring systems. All values represent actual measurements from the manufacturing trial of a single propeller component.

## 2.0 Technical Feasibility

### 2.1 Process Overview

The proposed manufacturing sequence comprises three distinct phases: powder production, SLM build operation, and finish machining. Each phase presents unique technical considerations that must be addressed for successful implementation.

Powder production utilizes gas atomization to convert solid steel billet into fine spherical powder suitable for the SLM process. This phase requires precise control of atomization parameters to achieve consistent powder morphology and size distribution. The subsequent SLM build process involves spreading thin layers of metal powder and selectively melting predetermined cross-sections using a high-power laser. Finally, finish machining removes the component from the build plate and achieves final dimensional tolerances through precision milling operations.

### 2.2 Material Specifications and Requirements

The manufacturing process utilizes ER70 steel, a low-carbon welding wire grade steel selected for its good weldability, mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance suitable for marine applications. Material inputs begin with solid billet that undergoes transformation into powder form before the additive manufacturing process.

**Powder Production Material Inputs**

| Material | Quantity |
|----------|----------|
| Steel Billet | 0.414 kg |
| Argon Gas | 2.58 kg |
| Cooling Water | 0.116 kg |

The gas atomization process converts 0.414 kg of steel billet into 0.352 kg of ER70 steel powder, representing a powder yield of approximately 85%. The balance constitutes fine powder outside the optimal size range for SLM processing, which is typically collected for alternative applications or recycling. The argon serves as both atomization gas and protective atmosphere, while water provides cooling for the atomization chamber.

For the SLM build phase, the entire 0.352 kg of produced powder is utilized, with a portion consumed in support structure formation. The powder characteristics including flowability, particle size distribution, and sphericity meet the requirements for reliable SLM processing based on preliminary testing.

### 2.3 Energy Consumption Analysis

Energy requirements represent a significant consideration in SLM implementation, with consumption occurring across multiple process stages. The total electrical energy input for manufacturing a single propeller measures 15.158 kWh, distributed across powder production, SLM build, and finish machining operations.

**Process Energy Consumption**

| Process Stage | Electricity Consumption |
|---------------|------------------------|
| Gas Atomization | 0.828 kWh |
| SLM Build | 11.49 kWh |
| Finish Machining | 2.84 kWh |
| **Total** | **15.158 kWh** |

The SLM build phase accounts for approximately 76% of total energy consumption, reflecting the extended processing time and high power requirements of the laser system, powder handling mechanisms, and environmental controls. The gas atomization energy intensity calculates to approximately 2 kWh per kg of powder produced, consistent with industry standards for small-scale atomization systems.

Contextual data indicates the SLM build operated for 6.55 hours with an average power consumption of 1.75 kW. The finish machining required 4.133 hours with an average power draw of 0.687 kW. These parameters provide baseline data for equipment sizing and operational planning.

Not all input material converts to usable product, with various loss mechanisms throughout the sequence.

The final propeller component weighs 0.204 kg, representing a material utilization efficiency of approximately 49% from initial billet to finished product. This efficiency factor compares favorably with traditional machining approaches for complex marine components, which often achieve 20-30% utilization rates for similar geometries.

The SLM process consumes 0.352 kg of steel powder including material utilized for support structures. The argon creates an inert atmosphere within the build chamber to prevent oxidation during processing, while compressed air serves multiple functions including powder bed cleaning and optical component cooling.

### 3.2 Utility Consumption

Beyond electrical energy, the manufacturing process requires several utilities that must be available in appropriate quantities and qualities. Gas consumption occurs in both powder production and SLM build phases, with different purposes and specifications for each application.

**Process Gas Requirements**

| Process Stage | Gas Type | Quantity |
|---------------|----------|----------|
| Gas Atomization | Argon | 2.58 kg |

The argon used in gas atomization must meet high purity standards (typically >99.995%) to prevent contamination of the powder material. For the SLM process, argon purity requirements are similarly stringent to ensure proper melt pool characteristics and mechanical properties in the final component. Compressed air specifications include oil-free operation with appropriate filtration to prevent powder contamination.

Cooling media requirements include 0.116 kg of water for the gas atomization chamber cooling system and 1.94 kg of cutting fluid for the finish machining operations. The cutting fluid serves both cooling and lubrication functions during the machining process, with specific formulation requirements for steel machining applications.

### 3.3 Output Characteristics and Waste Streams

The manufacturing process produces a single marine propeller weighing 0.204 kg as the primary product. Additionally, several waste streams require management, with the most significant being machined chips from the finish machining operation.

**Process Outputs**

| Output Type | Quantity |
|-------------|----------|
| Marine Propeller | 0.204 kg (1 unit) |

The machined chips result from removal of the 1 mm machining allowance plus support structure separation. Additional waste includes fine powder from the atomization process not captured in the optimal size fraction and powder degraded during the SLM process that cannot be reused.

The finished propeller dimensions and surface characteristics meet marine industry specifications for components of this size and application. Mechanical testing of sample components produced during trials indicates tensile strength, hardness, and dimensional accuracy within acceptable ranges for marine propulsion applications.

## 4.0 Operational Considerations

### 4.1 Process Timing and Productivity

The manufacturing sequence requires significant processing time, with the SLM build phase representing the longest single operation. Understanding these time requirements is essential for production planning and capacity assessment.

**Process Timing Analysis**

| Process Stage | Duration | Average Power |
|---------------|----------|---------------|
| SLM Build | 6.55 hours | 1.75 kW |
| Finish Machining | 4.133 hours | 0.687 kW |

The total hands-on manufacturing time exceeds 10 hours per propeller, though much of this represents unattended operation, particularly during the SLM build phase. The gas atomization process for powder production requires approximately 45 minutes based on the equipment capacity and powder quantity, though this operation can be batched to produce powder for multiple components.

Compared to traditional manufacturing approaches for similar marine propellers, the SLM process offers potential time savings in tooling and setup, though the actual build time may exceed machining time for conventional methods. For low-volume production or customized components, the reduced lead time for design changes may provide significant operational advantages.

### 4.2 Infrastructure Requirements

Successful implementation of the SLM manufacturing process requires specific infrastructure adaptations beyond standard machine shop capabilities. The specialized nature of the equipment and process demands careful consideration of facility requirements.

The SLM equipment necessitates stable foundation, vibration isolation, and controlled environment conditions including temperature and humidity stabilization. Electrical supply must accommodate peak power demands of approximately 8-10 kW during laser operation, with clean power free from surges or interruptions that could affect process stability.

Gas supply infrastructure must support the argon requirements for both atomization and SLM processes. For continuous operation, bulk argon storage or generator systems may be preferable to cylinder-based supply. Compressed air systems must provide adequate capacity and purity, potentially requiring additional filtration and drying equipment beyond standard shop air systems.

Powder handling requires dedicated areas with appropriate ventilation and fire suppression systems. The fine metal powders present explosion hazards that must be addressed through proper facility design and operational protocols. Finish machining operations can utilize standard CNC milling equipment with appropriate workholding solutions for the additively manufactured components.

## 5.0 Risk Assessment

### 5.1 Technical and Process Risks

The implementation of SLM technology for marine propeller manufacturing presents several technical risks that require mitigation strategies. Process stability represents a primary concern, as variations in powder characteristics, laser parameters, or environmental conditions can affect component quality and mechanical properties.

Powder quality consistency is essential for reproducible results. Variations in particle size distribution, morphology, or contamination can lead to defects in the final component. Implementation should include rigorous powder characterization and handling procedures to maintain consistency. Additionally, the gas atomization process requires precise control to achieve the necessary powder quality, with potential variations in yield and characteristics between batches.

The extended build time of 6.55 hours creates vulnerability to power interruptions or equipment malfunctions. Unplanned process stoppages can compromise component integrity and result in scrapped parts. Mitigation strategies include uninterruptible power supply systems, equipment redundancy where practical, and comprehensive preventive maintenance programs.

### 5.2 Resource and Supply Chain Risks

Dependence on specific material inputs and utilities creates supply chain vulnerabilities that must be addressed. Argon supply represents a particular concern, as interruptions would halt both powder production and SLM operations. The specialized nature of the steel powder may limit sourcing options and create lead time challenges during initial implementation.

Energy consumption intensity presents operational cost risks, particularly in regions with high electricity rates or unstable power supply. The total energy requirement of 15.158 kWh per component represents a significant cost factor that must be considered in economic evaluations. Fluctuations in energy pricing could substantially affect operating costs.

Material utilization efficiency, while improved compared to traditional methods, still results in significant material that does not become final product. The 0.116 kg of machined chips per propeller represents both a material cost and waste management consideration. Implementation of recycling protocols for both unused powder and machined chips can mitigate some of this impact.

### 5.3 Implementation and Operational Risks

The introduction of SLM technology requires specialized expertise that may not exist within traditional manufacturing organizations. The learning curve associated with process optimization, equipment maintenance, and troubleshooting presents implementation challenges. Comprehensive training programs and potentially external technical support arrangements should be included in implementation planning.

Regulatory compliance and certification represent additional considerations for marine components. Established standards and qualification procedures exist for traditionally manufactured propellers, but additive manufacturing may require development of new certification protocols. Engagement with classification societies and regulatory bodies early in the implementation process is essential to address these requirements.

## 6.0 Implementation Plan

### 6.1 Phased Implementation Approach

Successful adoption of SLM technology for marine propeller manufacturing recommends a phased implementation approach to manage risk and build organizational capability. The proposed implementation sequence includes four distinct phases: feasibility validation, pilot implementation, limited production, and full operational capability.

The feasibility validation phase, which this study supports, focuses on technical verification and process parameter optimization. This phase should include production of multiple test components for comprehensive mechanical testing and dimensional validation. The pilot implementation phase establishes basic operational capability with a single SLM system, developing standard operating procedures and training initial personnel.

Limited production phase expands capacity and begins supplying components for customer evaluation and qualification. This phase typically operates alongside existing manufacturing methods while building production experience and addressing any emerging issues. Full operational capability represents the transition to SLM as a primary manufacturing method, potentially with multiple systems and integrated powder production.

### 6.2 Equipment and Facility Requirements

Implementation requires procurement of several specialized equipment systems beyond standard machining capabilities. The core SLM equipment represents the most significant capital investment, with system selection based on build volume requirements, material capabilities, and operational features. Supporting equipment includes powder handling systems, post-processing equipment, and characterization instruments.

Facility modifications must address the specific requirements of additive manufacturing operations. Dedicated spaces for powder handling with appropriate ventilation and fire protection are essential. Environmental controls maintaining stable temperature and humidity improve process consistency. Electrical service upgrades may be necessary to support the power requirements, particularly if multiple SLM systems are planned.

The gas atomization equipment for powder production represents a significant additional investment. Alternatives include purchasing pre-alloyed powder from specialized suppliers, which may be preferable during initial implementation to reduce capital requirements and complexity.

### 6.3 Personnel and Training Requirements

The specialized nature of SLM technology necessitates development of specific technical expertise within the organization. Key roles include process engineers with expertise in additive manufacturing parameters, metallurgists understanding material behavior during the process, equipment technicians capable of maintaining and troubleshooting the systems, and operators trained in safe powder handling and equipment operation.

Training programs should combine external education with hands-on experience during the implementation phases. Partnerships with equipment suppliers, technical institutions, or consulting organizations can accelerate knowledge development. Documentation of procedures and lessons learned supports consistent operations and facilitates expansion of the capability.

Cross-training with traditional manufacturing personnel helps integrate the new technology within existing operations and leverages existing expertise in areas such as machining, quality control, and supply chain management. This integrated approach supports smoother implementation and organizational acceptance.

## 7.0 Recommendations

### 7.1 Technical Recommendations

Based on the feasibility assessment, the SLM process demonstrates technical capability for manufacturing ER70 steel marine propellers. Several technical recommendations emerge to optimize the process and address identified risks:

First, implement comprehensive powder management protocols including characterization, handling, and recycling procedures. Establish acceptance criteria for powder properties including particle size distribution, flow characteristics, and contamination levels. Develop standardized procedures for powder storage, handling, and recycling to ensure consistent quality and maximize material utilization.

Second, optimize process parameters to balance build rate, energy consumption, and component quality. The current parameters producing 11.49 kWh energy consumption over 6.55 hours represent a viable starting point, but further optimization may improve efficiency. Focus development efforts on reducing support structure requirements without compromising build success, as this directly affects material utilization and machining time.

Third, establish rigorous in-process monitoring and quality control procedures. Implement monitoring of critical parameters including laser power, scan speed, chamber atmosphere, and powder bed temperature. Develop non-destructive evaluation techniques specific to additively manufactured components to identify potential defects before costly machining operations.

### 7.2 Operational Recommendations

From an operational perspective, several recommendations support successful implementation:

Adopt a hybrid manufacturing approach that leverages the strengths of both additive and subtractive methods. Utilize SLM for producing the complex propeller geometry near net shape, followed by precision machining for critical surfaces and interfaces. This approach maximizes the benefits of both technologies while mitigating their individual limitations.

Develop a phased implementation plan that begins with lower-risk applications before progressing to critical components. Initial production could focus on prototypes, custom designs, or replacement parts where lead time advantages provide significant value. This staged approach builds operational experience and demonstrates capability before committing to high-volume production.

Establish strategic partnerships for powder supply during initial implementation to reduce capital investment and complexity. As production volume increases, evaluate the economic justification for in-house powder production capability based on material requirements, quality control needs, and cost considerations.

### 7.3 Strategic Recommendations

At a strategic level, the following recommendations guide successful technology adoption:

Position SLM capability as complementary to existing manufacturing methods rather than as a replacement. Identify specific applications where additive manufacturing provides distinct advantages such as design complexity, customization, or lead time reduction. This positioning minimizes organizational resistance and demonstrates value through specific use cases.

Engage with classification societies and regulatory bodies early in the implementation process to develop appropriate certification protocols for additively manufactured marine components. Proactive engagement helps shape developing standards and ensures compliance requirements are addressed from the beginning.

Invest in developing internal expertise through targeted hiring, training programs, and partnerships with research institutions. The specialized knowledge required for successful SLM implementation represents a significant barrier to entry that must be addressed through deliberate capability development.

## 8.0 Conclusion

The feasibility assessment demonstrates that Selective Laser Melting technology can successfully manufacture ER70 steel marine propellers meeting technical requirements for marine applications. The process sequence from powder production through finish machining produces functional components with acceptable material properties and dimensional accuracy.

Resource analysis indicates total energy consumption of 15.158 kWh per component, with the SLM build phase accounting for the majority at 11.49 kWh. Material utilization efficiency from initial billet to finished product measures approximately 49%, representing improvement over traditional subtractive methods for complex geometries like marine propellers. The process requires 0.414 kg of steel billet to produce a 0.204 kg propeller, with 0.116 kg of material removed as chips during finish machining.

Operational considerations include significant processing time exceeding 10 hours per component, specialized infrastructure requirements, and the need for developed technical expertise. Implementation risks center on process stability, supply chain dependencies, and the learning curve associated with new technology adoption.

The SLM process offers compelling advantages for marine propeller manufacturing including design freedom, customization capability, and reduced lead times for complex geometries. These benefits must be balanced against higher energy consumption, capital investment requirements, and operational complexity compared to conventional methods.

Based on the comprehensive assessment, Selective Laser Melting represents a technically feasible manufacturing approach for ER70 steel marine propellers. Implementation through a phased approach with appropriate risk mitigation strategies is recommended to build organizational capability and demonstrate value through specific applications before expanding to broader production.
